Ropes, drones and a cowboy can't catch Marshfield's escaped cattle

Jessica Trufant   The Patriot Ledger

Marshfield officials have beefed up their efforts to capture two elusive runaway cattle but they’re udderly stumped on how exactly to do so. The cow and bull are still on the run Monday, Nov. 9.

Ropes. Drones. Even a professional cowboy.

“It’s extremely difficult to catch them because of how unpredictable their behavior is, plus the size of them,” Police Chief Phil Tavares said. “They’re high-risk animals.”

The black angus bull and cow belong to a family on Main Street who have them for breeding and beef. Tavares said they aren’t pets and don’t have names.

They escaped their enclosure last Thursday and have been on the lam ever since. Despite dozens of sightings and not making it too far from home, Tavares said catching them is proving to be difficult.

Why the escaped Marshfield cattle are difficult to catch

“They’re a lot faster than I ever imagined,” said Tavares, who actually started his career as the town’s assistant animal control officer. “They can jump over things I never thought a cow could jump over.”

When cattle get separated from their herd, Tavares said they become highly stressed and aggressive, and chasing them just makes it worse. 

At one point, two officers had of them cornered between their two cruisers and the guardrail. One of the officers attempted to grab onto the cattle, which swiftly nudged him off and hopped the guardrail, Tavares said.

The public has been having a field day in the meantime, using artificial intelligence to create images of the cows at the local skate park, on the harbormaster's boat and a milk carton with a missing cow advertisement on it. There's even been talk of creating a fundraiser to pay for the bovine's freedom once captured so they avoid their fate.

But it's not all a laughing matter.

Why police are concerned about drivers

Tavares said the concerning part is that they're black, which makes it nearly impossible to see them in the dark. With Main Street having a speed limit of 45 miles per hour, he said the biggest concern is a motorist striking one of them.

But the two are now back together and hanging out in a wooded area away from the road, Tavares said.

How officials plan to capture the bull and cow

He said the best bet is to give the bull and cow space and attempt to bait them back to their property. A veterinarian is on call to tranquilize them if it comes to that, Tavares said. Either way, Tavares said they'll keep trying until the cows come home.

"Rushing the animal creates a higher risk. It has to be done slowly and methodically," Tavares said. "In the meantime, they're living their best lives."
