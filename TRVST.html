Interview: Evan George, Director of Communications, UCLA's STOP Methane Project

BY Selva Ozelli , Esq., CPA
PUBLISHED: 11·09·26
× ×
×
Listen to audio version of this article
Sponsored by

Instaread

Thanks partly to Turkey’s leadership, the First Lady Emine Erdogan, the Action Agenda for COP31 names “Zero Waste and Methane Reduction” as the first of 10 priorities for the summit.  For the first time in UN climate conference history, waste and circularity have been formally integrated as a core pillar of the COP agenda. Hosted in Antalya, Türkiye, from November 9 to 20, 2026, the Zero Waste and Methane Reduction initiative serves as one of the presidency's ten priority Action Agenda themes for COP31.

This creates a tension between Türkiye’s prominent diplomatic role as the host of COP31 and its position as Europe's primary waste destination, exposing a major environmental paradox. While global summits heavily emphasize curbing methane and municipal waste, the realities of the international waste trade are actively reshaping ecosystems with widespread implications to agricultural contamination, marine devastation, air and soil toxicity along the Turkish Mediterranean coast.

UCLA's STOP Methane Project

According to a report prepared by the "STOP Methane Project" of the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA), solid waste disposal sites are among the largest sources of methane emissions driving global warming. The upcoming COP31 provides a critical opportunity to address these global emissions.

UCLA's STOP Methane Project spotlights the 10 sites in Turkiye’s waste sector with the highest average methane emission rates as seen by a key satellite. These insights come from public data published by the independent nonprofit Carbon Mapper, leveraging Planet Labs’ Tanager-1 satellite. STOP Methane Project compiled this list of emitters by identifying the sites with the highest average emissions rates where at least two plumes were observed and quantified during a study period, which ran from January 1, 2025, to June 1, 2026. 

1. Tell us about UCLA's STOP Methane Project, run by the Emmett Institute on Climate Change and the Environment at the UCLA School of Law, which builds on the institute's broader Advancing Methane Regulation Project, that originally convened its foundational expert meetings in October 2023.

The Emmett Institute is based at the UCLA School of Law. We’re a leading environmental law center made up of environmental law professors and researchers with expertise in a wide range of topics. Since 2023, we have worked on methane reduction with a focus on how to use remote sensing to inform policy. In recent years the amount of satellite data has exploded. As our work has progressed, it became clear to us that there is an urgent need for a credible, academic source to spotlight the most important emissions data and the biggest mitigation opportunities, explain what it means, and make it easier for non-scientific actors to understand. 

STOP Methane, which is short for “spotlight on top plumes,” was launched in November 2025 the week before COP30 in Belém, Brazil to fill this need. This is our 1-year birthday! We help make the new data more prominent, understandable, and usable in guiding and motivating efforts to cut emissions. We do this by disseminating publicly available, science-based information about large emissions sources, in a form that’s easily understandable by legal and regulatory authorities, journalists and others, to provide accountability. By drawing attention to some big sources of methane we can make the invisible visible.

2. According to global emissions data, China, the United States, India, Brazil, and Russia are the world's largest top 5 methane emitters in the world. How does COP31 host country Turkiye rank in this regard, and how does Australia, which will be the COP31 negotiating country rank in this regard?

That’s a good question and worth exploring, however, UCLA’s STOP Methane Project has a narrower focus than total emissions of nations. It’s important for sure, it’s just not our area of expertise. What I can say about international comparisons is that when we publisheda list in spring of the 25 sites in the waste sector with the largest detected and quantified emissions rates worldwide, as seen by key satellites in 2025, the U.S., India, Brazil, and Turkiye were all on that list. Turkiye had two sites on that list, as did India. Brazil had three. When we published a similar global list for the oil and gas sector, the U.S. was on that list but not the others. This fall we also did a deep dive just on super-emitters from the biggest oil and gas basins in the U.S. because it’s such a notable player. 

3. What is methane pollution's impact on the environment and its contribution to climate change?

Methane is typically thought to be responsible for about 30% of global warming since pre-industrial times and is more than 80 times as powerful as carbon dioxide over a 20-year period. That’s why reducing methane is considered an “emergency brake” we can pull to slow climate change. Put another way, reducing methane emissions is just about the fastest way to drive down global temperatures and where it used to be hard to detect and difficult to measure, it is now much easier to do so thanks to the satellite technology. That’s why this is so important, There’s also a benefit to public health, because where there’s methane — at least in the oil and gas and solid waste sectors — there are often harmful co-pollutants such as benzene.

4. What was your goal in publishing 10 sites in Turkiye’s waste sector with the highest average methane emission rates as seen by a key satellite Tanager constellation ahead of COP31? 

A key goal of UCLA’s STOP Methane Project is to draw attention to some of the sources with the largest observed average emission rates of methane as seen by key satellites, because they can be big opportunities to drive down emissions. Landfill methane is not usually headline news but when COP host Türkiye named ‘zero waste and methane reduction’ as a top priority for COP 31 that laid the groundwork for a really robust set of conversations about methane at the upcoming summit. There are dozens, perhaps more than a hundred, side events dedicated to the topic. We highlighted landfills in Türkiye in Fall 2026 to contribute to Türkiye’s ongoing and very promising dialogue about how to turn this data into action. As with all our reports, the goal is to help inform communities and policymakers about opportunities to reduce methane and whatever co-pollutants might also be emitted from these sites.  It also allows operators who are potentially responsible for those emissions to respond to the information and hopefully consider possible mitigation opportunities.   

5.  In a recent interview with TRVST, Dr. Nusret Demir indicated that "Landfills are one of the largest human sources of methane, a gas with roughly 80 times the warming power of CO₂ over 20 years. Satellites have changed this field. Sentinel-5P/TROPOMI tracks methane globally, and high-resolution instruments such as GHGSat, NASA's EMIT, and Carbon Mapper can pinpoint individual plumes at facility level." Tell us about the Tanager satellite constellation and how it tracks global methane emmissions.

The Tanager-1 satellite, by Planet Labs and Carbon Mapper, has been considered one of the most sophisticated methane-detection satellites in the world and this fall they launched Tanager-2 to expand the capabilities for research like ours. It was pretty exciting to watch the recent launch in real time on a live feed. I’m not a scientist, but my understanding is that both Tanager satellites are designed and optimized to detect methane super-emitters with high granularity so they can be traced to a specific location and acted on quickly. Carbon Mapper, which is a science-based nonprofit based in Pasadena, not too far from UCLA, makes the satellite data available through its data portal and also provides analysis of the average emission rate of these satellite observations. 

6. Tell us about the 10 waste sites in Turkiye, linked to 70 plumes observed by the Tanager satellite.

We identified the 10 sites in Turkey’s waste sector with the highest average emission rates of methane observed by the Tanager‑1 satellite during the study period from January 1, 2025, to June 1, 2026, from among all sites with at least two quantified plumes. These are mostly modern landfills. Together, two operators are identified as potentially responsible for more than half of the Top 10 sites: İSTAÇ, a waste‑management subsidiary of the Istanbul Metropolitan Municipality, and ITC, a waste‑management and landfill‑gas energy company. You can see the list and explore the map here.

7. Will your team attend COP31?  If so, tell us about your platform/event.

Yes, three of us from the UCLA team will be on the ground in Antalya and we are excited to engage with others in this space. We’ll be participating in four panel discussions in Week One, including the following:

  • Monday, November 9 (13:30-14:30 pm): “Methane, Air, and Health: From Detection to Community Benefits,” COP31 Presidency Zero Waste Pavillion Key Partner Stage. Organizer: Global Methane Hub
  • Tuesday, November 10, (9-9:45 am): “From Satellites to Action: Turning Global Landfill Methane Data into Climate Policy and Impact,” COP31 Presidency Zero Waste Pavillion Side Events Stage. Organizer: Global Methane Hub.
  • Wednesday, November 11, (15:25–16:25): "From Methane Data to Public Action," Panel Room of the YÖK Turkey in the Blue Zone Pavilion. Organizer: Latin American Faculty of Social Sciences.
8.  Anything else you would like to add.

Because global methane reduction is so urgent and so promising, there is a really robust community of nonprofits, research organizations, NGOs and philanthropists doing amazing work in this space all around the world. UCLA’s team is grateful to play a small part in this much-larger universe of changemakers. If you are interested in helping to ‘pull the emergency brake,’ there are lots of ways to get involved.  

9. How can people reach you?

If people want to follow our reports and learn more about putting satellite data into policy action, they can subscribe to our Substack and reach us through that site or reach out to me directly. We are a small but growing team and I’m the communications lead for the project.

Glossary Terms:

Methane
Potent greenhouse gas from farms and fossil fuels; traps heat.
Contamination
Unwanted materials that make recyclables or environments impure.
Landfill
Designated area for waste disposal; compacts and buries trash.
